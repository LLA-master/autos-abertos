Detaining someone before trial is the most violent thing a State under the rule of law does as a matter of routine. That is why the law surrounds the act with requirements, and why the technical part of a ruling like that of 3 March deserves to be read by people who are not lawyers: that is where one sees whether the judge skipped steps or not. This chapter is about the technique. It has no WhatsApp messages. It has articles of law, precedents and, at the end, a set of instructions on handcuffs, custody hearings and what to do with a detainee who is a lawyer. It is the dullest part and the most important.

## The two Latin phrases

Criminal procedure works with two Latin expressions that can be translated without losing anything. The first is fumus commissi delicti, the "smoke of the crime": indications that the crime took place and that the suspect took part in it. The second is periculum libertatis, the "danger of liberty": a showing that, at large, the suspect hinders the investigation, flees, keeps offending or disturbs public order. Without the first, there is no reason to detain. Without the second, there is no reason to detain now.

Article 312 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, which the ruling transcribes, lists four possible grounds for the danger: safeguarding public order, safeguarding economic order, the convenience of the criminal investigation and the enforcement of the criminal law. The justice says one is enough. And he says he found three, "cumulatively".

On the smoke, the ruling refers back to the preceding 79 items, where it described the conduct attributed to each suspect, and concludes that there are "strong indications that the individuals named above organised a complex structure for the commission of crimes with a profoundly negative repercussion on society". On the danger, it organises the argument in three lettered blocks, like someone who wants to be checked.

For the criminal investigation: the suspects' "wide network of connections", the "indications of threats against people who cross the group's interests", the "continuous use of mechanisms to hide the tracks" and the "high likelihood of elimination and manipulation of documents and evidence". For public order: the "need for social pacification through the creation of a feeling in society that the justice system responds swiftly to an offence of extremely high social repercussion, of billion-real dimensions", the "risk of repeat offending" and the "subjective reach of the offences", which "affected the lives of millions of Brazilians and the credibility of financial institutions". For the enforcement of the criminal law: the indications that the practices continue, the money laundering and the "concealment and dissipation of the assets obtained unlawfully".

A sceptical reader will notice the first public-order reason: "a feeling in society of a swift response". It is the most controversial argument in the theory of pre-trial detention, because detention is not meant to give a response, it is meant to protect the proceedings. The justice knows this and does not leave it on its own: he surrounds it with precedents. He cites Second Panel rulings by Justices Gilmar Mendes and Edson Fachin, an opinion by Nunes Marques in a case where he wrote the panel's judgment, on "the concrete gravity of the crimes alleged, the petitioner's significant role in the complex criminal organisation, his power of influence revealed in the record and the concrete and reasonable risk of repeat offending", and a line of decisions, from Toffoli to Celso de Mello, to the effect that detention is legitimate "when the need to interrupt or reduce the activity of members of a criminal organisation is shown". The thesis, therefore, is not that one detains to calm public opinion; it is that one detains to interrupt an organisation which, according to the record, was still operating.

## Contemporaneity

There is a requirement that recent case law has added and that the ruling tackles head-on: the danger must be current. One does not detain today for a risk from two years ago. Item 93 answers: "the criminal organisation shows a very high capacity for reorganisation, even after operations have been launched", and "the criminal activities, as demonstrated by the Federal Police in its request, continued to occur even after the opening of the inquiry and the operations arising from it". The anchor here is the facts of chapter 1: the release of November 2025, the billion-real freeze of January 2026, the "Projeto DV" of December. If the PF is right about them, the requirement is met; if it is not, that is where the defence will come in.

Item 92 rules out the alternatives: "the less severe measures provided for in our legal system are not, in relation to these suspects, capable of averting the risk scenario". And it gives the reason that unifies the ruling: "allowing them to remain at liberty means keeping in operation a criminal organisation that has already caused billions in damage to society". Note the verb: "keeping in operation". The ruling treats the four detainees as parts of a machine in operation, not as defendants for a past crime.

## The staircase

From there the ruling walks down a staircase of proportionality, and the staircase is perhaps its most defensible feature. Step one: four people detained, those to whom the PF attributes "violent and repeated conduct". Step two: four people with ankle monitors, those whose conduct "is of similar gravity" but who "would not be involved in the armed cell". The justice transcribes article 319 in full, with its nine measures, and picks five for the officials and three for the others, always with the clause that detention "can be replaced by less severe measures" at that moment and may return. Step three: five companies suspended, with the speech on free enterprise from the previous chapter, which serves precisely to say that suspension is the exception.

It is a ruling that knows it will be read by a court. And it was: item 125 sends the case to the Second Panel for confirmation, as the Court's rules require for a reporting justice's precautionary orders in criminal matters. The Panel confirmed it unanimously in a virtual session between 13 and 20 March, as recorded in a ruling of 31 March which, for that very reason, declares moot the internal appeal by Vorcaro's defence against the detention. The appellate path within the Supreme Court itself, therefore, closed in seventeen days.

## How to arrest

What gets the least comment and says the most about the author is in items 112 to 117. The ruling orders that the warrants be executed "in a calm, respectful and discreet manner, without any spectacle", praises the Federal Police for having acted that way "on previous occasions" and orders compliance with Binding Precedent 11, which is the Supreme Court's rule on handcuffs: only where there is a risk of flight or danger, with written justification. For suspects who are lawyers, it orders the Bar Statute to be applied, which provides for detention in a "staff-officer's room" (*sala de Estado-Maior*), and the local chapter of the Bar to be notified.

Then comes the custody hearing, which must take place within 24 hours before the federal judge of the place where the detainee is held. And here the ruling does something worth recording: it delegates and limits. The custody judge may check "the strictly formal requirements of the arrest and of the treatment given to the detainee", but may not "review the grounds that led to its being ordered, nor even decide against keeping the person in custody". If he sees an irregularity, he informs the reporting justice. "Any decision to release on grounds of irregularity in the execution of custody may be taken only by the reporting justice of this case." In plain words: the first-instance judge who receives Daniel Vorcaro in handcuffs may check whether he was treated well; release him, no. Some will see excessive centralisation in this; others, the logic of a case that runs at the Supreme Court. The ruling does not discuss, it orders.

It closes with logistics: detention in "an establishment compatible with the personal condition of the suspects", with physical integrity, a lawyer and visits; letters to the commercial registries and to the tax authority (Receita Federal) about the suspended companies; a letter to the Central Bank; and an order that explains why nobody knew anything beforehand: "after the effective execution of all the measures granted herein", let the ruling be made public and the lawyers be given access to the file. Sealed until the knock on the door. Then, light.

## What remains

A 48-page precautionary ruling is, at bottom, a reasoned bet. The justice bet that the Federal Police is right about the crew, about the officials and about the continuation of the acts, and explained every step of the bet, with law and precedent. What it does not do, because it cannot, is prove. Proof belongs to the trial that comes afterwards and which, when the STF opened the record to the public, did not yet exist. Anyone who wants to know whether the bet was right has to wait. Anyone who wants to know whether it was well built can read the 48 pages today. This chapter tried to show where to look.

> Reading rule number five: in a pre-trial detention, what is discussed is not "did he do it?", it is "at large, does he get in the way?". The two questions have different answers, different evidence and different judges.
